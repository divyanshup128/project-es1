Learn: labour law in India, start to now

From the first Factories Act to the four Labour Codes. Browse the timeline, search for a specific law, or open any entry for what changed between the old Act and the new Code, and how it plays out in practice.

The big picture
Where labour law comes from

The Constitution treats work as a matter of dignity. Four Fundamental Rights touch it: equal opportunity in public jobs (Art. 16), the freedom to form unions (Art. 19), the ban on forced labour (Art. 23), and the ban on children working in factories, mines and hazardous jobs (Art. 24).

The Directive Principles (Arts. 39, 41, 42, 43 and 43A) tell the state to protect workers\u2019 health and strength, keep children from being abused, secure just and humane conditions and maternity relief, work towards a living wage, and involve workers in management.

Labour is on the Concurrent List, so both Parliament and state legislatures can make labour laws. That is why some laws are enforced by the Centre, some by states, and some by both, and why the same rule can differ between states.

Two aims of labour policy

Labour policy has always pulled two ways: industrial peace and worker welfare. Recent policy adds attracting investment, creating jobs, social security for unorganised workers, social security cards, and better use of welfare board funds.

What the Codes changed, at a glance
  • One wage definition across all four Codes. Allowances excluded from wages are capped, so basic plus other wage components must reach 50% of total pay. This feeds PF, gratuity and bonus.
  • Minimum wage floor for all workers, and wages paid on time.
  • Fixed-term employment is formally recognised, with benefits such as gratuity after a shorter qualifying period.
  • Gig and platform workers are recognised for the first time, with aggregators contributing towards their social security.
  • Thresholds moved: factories 20 workers with power and 40 without (was 10 and 20); contract-labour rules from 50 workers (was 20); standing orders and prior-permission rules for retrenchment and closure from 300 workers (was 100).
  • Working hours: 8 a day and 48 a week stay, and a four-day week is possible inside that cap.
  • Safety: free annual health check-ups for workers aged 40 and above, and appointment letters for all workers.
  • Strikes: advance-notice rules now apply across establishments.

Status: in force from 21 November 2025. Final Central Rules notified 8 May 2026. State Rules vary, so confirm what your state has notified.

Unfair labour practices

Under the old Industrial Disputes Act, no employer, worker or union (registered or not) may commit an unfair labour practice. The Fifth Schedule lists them.

By employers:

  • Threatening dismissal, lock-out or closure to stop workers joining a union.
  • Buying off a union drive with timed pay rises, setting up a company-sponsored union, or favouring one union over another.
  • Punishing workers for union activity: dismissal, lower seniority, refused promotions, or discharging office bearers.
  • Victimising workers who take part in a lawful strike.

By workers and unions: coercing others to join or stay out, advising or taking part in illegal strikes, and intimidation. The Industrial Relations Code keeps the idea; check its schedule for the current list.

Women at work
  • Equal pay: the Equal Remuneration Act is now part of the Code on Wages, which bars gender discrimination in wages and in recruitment.
  • Maternity: the Maternity Benefit Act (now in the Social Security Code) gives paid leave, with 26 weeks for the first two children, and a crèche duty for larger employers.
  • Night work and hazards: older rules limited women\u2019s night shifts in factories; under the Codes women may work at night with consent and safety measures set by the government.
  • Harassment: the POSH Act, 2013 requires an Internal Committee in workplaces with 10 or more employees. Use Find My Issue if this is your situation.
A good employment agreement

An employment agreement spells out who does what and for how much. Look for these ten points in yours: parties, start date, job title and description, place of work, hours, probation, salary, restrictive terms, holidays, and deductions, expenses and notice period.

  • Get the policies the contract refers to, in writing. Vague references to \u201Cpolicies\u201D are a warning sign.
  • Check whether you count as a \u201Cworkman\u201D or \u201Cemployee\u201D, because the protections differ.
  • State law applies as well, so the terms should respect your state\u2019s rules.
  • Insist on a proper appointment letter with the terms set out.
  • Confidentiality and notice clauses bind you too, so read them before you sign.
Landmark rulings worth knowing
  • Apprentices: in UP State Road Transport Corp. v. UP Parivahan Nigam Shishukh Berozgar Sangh (1995), the Supreme Court held that a trained apprentice should get preference over outside candidates, and need not come through the employment exchange.
  • Contract labour: in Steel Authority of India v. National Union Waterfront Workers (2001), a five-judge bench held that abolishing contract labour does not automatically make those workers permanent. But if the contract arrangement is a sham, they are treated as the principal employer\u2019s own employees.
  • Licences: the Gujarat High Court in Food Corporation of India Workers\u2019 Union held that contract labour is lawful only through a licensed contractor and a registered principal employer. If either is missing, workers are deemed employed by the principal employer.
  • Gratuity: the Supreme Court in Delhi Cloth and General Mills (1970) described gratuity as a reward for service, not a gift to be withheld at will. The Bombay High Court in Bombay Gas Co. v. Papa Akbar (1990) held that forfeiting gratuity needs the service to have been terminated, not just a strike.

These rulings were decided under the older Acts. The Codes carry the same ideas forward, but check whether a ruling still applies to your case.
